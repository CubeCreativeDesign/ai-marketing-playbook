# Mechanical Style Rules (Marketing Copy)

The consistency layer. This handles the boring decisions a writer shouldn't have to re-make every time: numbers, dates, money, abbreviations, formatting. It does **not** cover voice, tone, or word choice. Those live in the anti-AI writing rules and run alongside this.

House defaults are set below. Three of them are judgment calls flagged as `[HOUSE]` so they're easy to find and change. Examples use generic B2B service-business framing; swap them for your vertical's specifics when you instantiate this template.

---

## Contractions

`[HOUSE]` **Use them.** Contractions make the text easier to read. Use them
across the board, in all the text.

Write `don't`, `it's`, `you're`, `we're`, `that's`, `here's`, `isn't`, `won't`,
`can't`, `you'll`, `we've`. It's how the reader talks and it reads faster on a phone,
which is where most of them are.

Three places to keep the long form:

- **When the sentence turns on the negative.** "We do not have a clean cause for
  either" lands harder than "We don't." If the whole point of the line is the *not*,
  spell it out.
- **In a legal or pricing commitment.** "There is no setup fee and no exit fee" is a
  promise. Contractions make a promise sound casual.
- **Where two contractions collide.** "It's not that we're saying you shouldn't" is a
  mouthful. Expand one of them.

Don't overcorrect either. A page with no contractions at all reads like a brochure
written by a committee, which is the thing the voice rules exist to avoid.

Two mistakes a find-and-replace makes, both caught in the 2026-09-21 pass:

- **`have` is only an auxiliary in front of a past participle.** "Sites we have access
  to" is possession. "We've access to" is not American English.
- **`is` does not contract before `to`.** "How hard it is to reach" must not become
  "how hard it's to reach."

## Numbers

`[HOUSE]` **Prose:** spell out one through nine, use numerals for 10 and up.
**Headlines, list titles, stats, and "X ways/tips/reasons" formats:** always numerals.

| Context | Write |
|---|---|
| Casual prose | "We serve three counties." |
| Headline or list | "7 Questions to Ask Before Hiring an Agency" |
| Any stat or data point | "cut bounce rate by 4%" |
| Start of a sentence | spell out, or rewrite ("Twelve clients..." or reword) |
| Ages | numerals always: "a 5-year-old company" |
| Measurements | numerals always: "a 6-foot booth display" |

Never start a sentence with a numeral.

## Units and measurements

- Numerals plus a spelled-out unit in prose: `6 feet`, `3 inches`, `10 square feet`, `50 gallons`.
- Temperatures: `72 degrees`. `72°F` is OK in a table.
- Hyphenate a measurement used as a modifier: `6-foot perimeter`, `10-gallon tank`.
- Abbreviate units (`ft.`, `sq. ft.`) only in tables.

## Percentages

Numerals plus the `%` sign everywhere, body copy included: `5%`, `40%`. This is AP style since 2019, so no deviation is needed.

- Repeat the sign in a range: `15% to 20%`.
- Put a leading zero under 1%: `0.6%`.
- A change between two percentages is in **percentage points**: 55% to 50% is a drop of 5 percentage points (a 9% decrease).
- Casual use spells it out: "a zero percent chance."
- Never open a sentence with a numeral. Reword so it doesn't.

## Money

- Numerals always: `$5`, `$1,200`, `$3 million`.
- Drop `.00` on whole dollars: `$50`, not `$50.00`.
- Spell out and abbreviate large round figures the readable way: `$3 million`, not `$3,000,000`.
- Ranges: `$200 to $400` in prose, `$200–$400` in tables or tight space.

## Dates

- Months with a specific date: abbreviate Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., Dec. Spell out March, April, May, June, July.
- Month with year only, no date: spell out. "September 2026."
- No ordinals on dates: `Oct. 14`, not `Oct. 14th`.
- Years as numerals. Decades: `the 2010s` or `the '90s`.

**Evergreen by default.** Most posts stay live for years, so avoid time words that go stale: "this year," "recently," "last month," "currently," "new" (for a feature), "the latest." Use the real year or date: "in 2026," "as of September 2026." A timely or news post may use relative time words when the piece justifies it. "New" may lead a headline when the thing really is new and the body gives the date. Two rules hold either way: a stat always carries its year, and seasons stay lowercase (spring, summer termite swarms).

## Times

- Figures with `a.m.`/`p.m.`, lowercase with periods: `9 a.m.`, `2:30 p.m.`
- Drop `:00` on the hour: `9 a.m.`, not `9:00 a.m.`
- Use "noon" and "midnight," not `12 p.m.`/`12 a.m.`
- Ranges: `9 a.m. to 5 p.m.` or `9–11 a.m.`

## Days of the week

Capitalize, don't abbreviate in body copy. Abbreviate only in tables or schedules.

## Phone numbers and addresses (NAP consistency)

**Scope: client-site builds, not blog body copy.** Blog posts in the pipeline carry no phone numbers or addresses, so the writing stages do not apply this rule. It stays canon here for site-build work and is deliberately left off Stage 2's apply list.

This matters more for client sites than anything else on this list. Phone, address, and business name should match **exactly** across the site, footer, contact page, and Google Business Profile. Inconsistency hurts local rankings and trust.

- **Phone:** pick one format and lock it site-wide. Recommended: `(336) 555-0142`. Don't mix `336.555.0142` and `336-555-0142` on the same site.
- **Addresses:** numerals for street numbers: `123 Main St.` Abbreviate St., Ave., Blvd. only with a full street address. Spell them out when standalone ("on Main Street").
- **States:** spell out in body copy ("based in North Carolina"). Two-letter postal codes (NC) only with a full mailing address.

## State names

`[HOUSE]` This deliberately deviates from AP's always-spell-out rule, so the page matches both ways people search.

- **Headings (H1 to H3):** always the full name. "HVAC Marketing in North Carolina."
- **Body copy:** the full name on first mention, then the two-letter code, sparingly. "North Carolina... NC operators."
- **Title tags and meta descriptions:** the code is OK when space is tight.
- **Full mailing addresses and tables:** the two-letter code.
- Pair a city with its state on first mention, unless AP lets the city stand alone (Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas, and similar).

## Abbreviations and acronyms

- Spell out on first reference, then abbreviate: "Google Business Profile (GBP)... the GBP listing."
- Skip the parenthetical if the acronym is more familiar than the full term (SEO, DIY, FAQ). Don't write "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)" for a general audience.
- No periods in most: SEO, FBI, NATO, DIY.

## Hyphens

Hyphenate two or more words that work together as one adjective **before** a noun: `full-service company`, `first-time customer`, `6-foot perimeter`, `well-known brand`, `higher-income families`, `best-rated company`, `half-hour inspection`.

- Don't hyphenate after the noun: "the company is full service."
- Never after an `-ly` word or "very": `highly rated tech`, `very good results`.
- A familiar open compound that can't be misread takes no hyphen, even before a noun: `air conditioning repair`, `small business owner`, `real estate agent`. A proper noun takes none either: `New York office`.
- Most prefixes take no hyphen: `nonprofit`, `preexisting`, `multistep`. `self-`, `all-`, and `ex-` do: `self-service`.
- Suspended hyphens for pairs: `5- and 6-year-olds`.

## Lists

- Introduce a list with a short lead-in and a colon.
- Capitalize the first word of every item.
- End an item with a period only if it's a full sentence. Fragments get no end punctuation.
- No commas or semicolons at the end of items.
- Keep items parallel: all start with a verb, or all are nouns.
- Number a list only when order matters (steps). Otherwise use bullets.
- Keep a list to 3 to 7 items.

## Tables

Tables must read on a phone, where they get tight fast.

- Keep each cell to a number, a word, or a short phrase (8 words max). No full sentences.
- Column headers are 1 to 3 words.
- Aim for 3 or 4 columns, 6 at most. If you need more, split it into two tables.
- Abbreviations are OK in tables only: `ft.`, `sq. ft.`, `NC`, `%`, `$1.2M`, `Mon–Fri`.
- No links, bullets, or bold inside cells.
- Put any explanation in a sentence above or below the table.

## Punctuation

- `[HOUSE]` **Oxford comma: keep it.** "design, development, and support."
- **Em dashes:** `[HOUSE]` **at most 1 per 300 words in customer copy** . Each one has to earn its place. When in doubt, split the sentence into a period, a comma, or parentheses. Never use `--` or a spaced hyphen as a stand-in. scripts/qc.sh warns past 1 per 300 words of main body. A warning, never a fail.
- **Exclamation points:** `[HOUSE]` a judgment call, not a hard rule. Default to at most one per piece. Use another only when a line truly calls for it. Never two in a row (`!!`). A justified `!` is OK in a heading. The QC scan doesn't check this.
- **Ampersands:** write "and," not `&`, in body copy and headings, unless `&` is part of a brand name (Texas A&M).
- **Slashes:** avoid "and/or" and "his/her." Pick a word or rewrite.
- **etc.:** don't end a list with it. Name the items or say "and more."
- **versus:** spell it out in body copy. `vs.` is OK in headlines and tables.
- **Quotation marks:** periods and commas inside. Colons and semicolons outside. Question marks inside only if part of the quote.
- **Ranges:** "to" in prose ("Monday to Friday"), en dash in tables/tight space ("Mon–Fri").

## Capitalization

**Headlines and subheads: AP title case, every time.** No exceptions, no sentence case, no switching by content type. This pipeline uses H1–H3 only, so it applies to the H1, all H2 and H3 subheads, page titles, and section headers.

The rules:

- Capitalize the first and last word, always, regardless of what they are.
- Capitalize all words four letters or longer.
- Capitalize short principal words too, even under four letters: nouns, pronouns, verbs, adverbs, adjectives, and subordinating conjunctions (because, while, although, if). "Why Your Funnel Is Broken," not "Why Your Funnel is Broken."
- Lowercase these when they fall mid-title only: articles (a, an, the), coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet), and prepositions of three letters or fewer (to, of, in, on, at, by, up).
- Any of those lowercase words gets capitalized when it is the first or last word.
- Capitalize both parts of a hyphenated compound: "Full-Service Marketing," not "Full-Service marketing."

The two that trip people up:

- "To" stays lowercase even in an infinitive: "How to Choose a Marketing Agency."
- Short prepositions go lowercase mid-headline: "Signs of Trouble in Your Sales Pipeline."

Examples:

> 5 Questions to Ask Before Hiring an Agency
> What to Do When Your Leads Dry Up
> The Best Time of Year to Audit Your Website

**Heading punctuation:**

- No period at the end of an H1, H2, or H3.
- A question mark is fine when the heading is a real question: "How Long Does a Termite Treatment Last?"
- `!` is OK when the heading justifies it (see Punctuation).
- Avoid colons in H2 and H3 subheads. Save them for the H1 or title if needed. **One exception: the intro H2, `## Introduction: <headline>`.** The pipeline needs it. Automation reads the word "Introduction" plus the colon as the marker, and the text after the colon is the headline.
- **Headings promise something**. The H1 and title state a benefit, a result, or a named problem, not a topic label. "Why Leads Drop After a Site Redesign" passes. "Marketing Tips for Service Businesses" fails. Each H2 makes the reader want the section: if the subhead could head any post on the topic, rewrite it. A reviewer checks this. No script can.
- **Lead with the point**. The intro's first sentence is 12 words or fewer and names the reader's problem or the takeaway. No throat-clearing: no "Have you ever wondered", no scene-setting, no dictionary definition. QC warns past 12 words.
- **Length follows the message**. Never cut a section only to make the post shorter. Cut it when it fails "so what?" for the reader or carries no proof. The brief's word count is a guide, not a cap to trim toward.
- **Use the reader's own words**. Write the terms readers use in calls, reviews, and searches, not marketing terms: owners say "leads," "jobs," and "the phone ringing".
- **Name the reader early**. Describe the reader by role and situation in the first 75 words, for example "an owner running a five-person crew". A reviewer checks this.
- **Raise the objection, admit a limit**. In a B2B post, give the reader's top objection its own paragraph (cost, time, "we tried that," "my nephew does our site") and answer it with proof. Early in every post, state one real limit: who the tactic won't help, what it costs, or how long it takes. Admitting a limit makes every other claim easier to believe.
- **Reader first, strong verbs**. "You" and "your" should outnumber "we," "our," and "us": the reader is the hero, not [YOUR_AGENCY]. Don't open sentences with "There is," "There are," or "It is" (more than 3 per post gets a warning). Cut "very" and "really" and use a stronger word. QC warns on all three.
- No quotation marks around a whole heading.

**Job titles:** capitalize before a name ("Owner Mike Davis"), lowercase after or standalone ("Mike Davis, the owner").

Don't capitalize words for emphasis in body copy. Bold or restructure instead.

## Link text

- Link the words that describe where the link goes: "see our termite inspection checklist." Never "click here," "read more," or a bare URL.
- Don't include a leading article (a, the, our) or trailing punctuation in the link.
- Keep anchors 2 to 6 words.
- Never use the same anchor text for two different pages in one post.

Anchor text tells Google what the linked page is about, so this is an SEO rule as well as a readability one.

## Emphasis

- Bold a key phrase so a skimmer catches it: at most one bolded phrase or one key sentence per section, never a whole paragraph.
- Don't use italics for emphasis. Screen readers and dyslexic readers struggle with them.
- Never underline. Underlined text looks like a link.
- Never use ALL CAPS for emphasis.
- Don't stack styles (no bold italics).

## Brand and product names

- Spell brand names the way the company does: `WordPress`, `Joomla`, `HubSpot`, `iPhone`, `eBay`.
- Capitalize a brand at the start of a sentence, even an eBay-style one. Rewrite if it looks odd.
- Never put `™` or `®` in body copy.
- Don't turn a brand into a verb or a generic word unless that's standard usage: "search on Google," not "google it."

## Usage pairs

- **fewer** (things you count) versus **less** (amounts): "fewer callbacks," "less time."
- **that** (a clause the sentence needs, no comma) versus **which** (an extra clause, with commas).
- **affect** (verb) versus **effect** (noun).
- **who** for people, **that** for things.
- "More than" and "over" are both fine with numbers (AP since 2014).
- Write "for example" and "that is," not e.g. and i.e.

## Web and product terms (settled spellings)

`website` (one word), `email` (no hyphen), `homepage`, `login` (noun) / `log in` (verb), `online`, `internet` (lowercase), `ecommerce`, `nonprofit`. Keep these consistent so they don't drift across a 300-page pipeline.

### Quick gut-check before publishing

- Numbers follow the threshold rule
- Phone and address match the GBP listing exactly
- Acronyms spelled out on first use
- All headlines and subheads in AP title case
- Em dashes: 1 per 300 words at most
- Tables fit a phone: short cells, 6 columns max
- Links use descriptive anchor text, never "click here"
- The H1 promises a benefit, result, or named problem, and no H2 could head any post
- The intro opens with the problem or takeaway in 12 words or fewer

**Noun or adjective versus verb:** `sign-up` / `sign up`, `follow-up` / `follow up`, `setup` / `set up`, `checkout` / `check out`, `opt-in` / `opt in`, `how-to` (noun). Also: `call to action` (CTA after first use), `Wi-Fi`, `smartphone`, `URL`, `PDF`, `Google Business Profile`, `Search Console`. Platform names as the companies write them: `LinkedIn`, `YouTube`, `TikTok`, `Facebook`, `X (formerly Twitter)`.

## Industry settled spellings

`[HOUSE]` **The industry's own spelling beats AP when the reader is in the industry** (B2B: owners, operators, administrators). For **B2C** pieces aimed at homeowners or parents, mix them: use the industry spelling first, plus the consumer variant people actually search for.

- Add the vertical's industry spellings here during setup.
