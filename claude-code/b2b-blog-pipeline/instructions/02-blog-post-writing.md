## Research and Verification

### Before Writing, You Must:
1. Conduct web searches on the topic to gather current data, trends, and expert insights
2. Identify key statistics with their original sources
3. Verify all statistics and claims through multiple authoritative sources
4. Focus on recent data (within 2-3 years) unless historical context is needed
5. Prioritize sources: [YOUR_VERTICAL] trade publications, industry associations, relevant government agencies, university research programs, home services industry research

### Credibility Standard — CRITICAL
[YOUR_VERTICAL] business owners are hands-on, no-nonsense operators. They will fact-check claims, recognize inflated numbers, and dismiss content that doesn't reflect their reality. Every statistic, benchmark, and claim must be:
- Verifiable from a specific, credible source
- Realistic for the company size tier being targeted
- Presented honestly without exaggeration or rounding that changes the meaning

If you cannot verify a specific number, use broader trend language instead. A vague but honest statement is always better than a precise but fabricated one.

### Google Deep Research Integration
If research was provided in Stage 1:
- Cross-reference the draft against research findings
- Identify gaps where research covers something the draft does not
- Verify that statistics match the research sources
- Add relevant data points from research that strengthen the argument

### Handling `unverified` items from the Stage 0 bundle

Stage 0's Phase E tags every quote and statistic it could not confirm against its source
page as `unverified`. Treat that tag as **blocking**:

- Never state an `unverified` number as fact.
- Either verify it yourself per the rules below, replace it with a verified equivalent, or
  drop it and use broader trend language.
- Never launder it by rewording ("studies suggest", "roughly") — the problem is that nobody
  confirmed the number, and rewording hides that instead of fixing it.
- If a draft would be materially weaker without it, say so in your Stage 2 notes rather
  than shipping the claim.

### Source Verification Rules — BLOCKING, not aspirational

- EVERY link must be verified as working AND containing the cited statistic.
- "Verified" means: you personally loaded the URL in a browser during Stage 2 and saw the specific number or quote on the rendered page. Reading a Deep Research report or a secondary summary is NOT verification.
- Do not fabricate or assume statistics. If you cannot personally confirm the stat on the linked page, either (a) remove the stat and use broader trend language, or (b) find a different primary source and verify the stat on IT before citing.
- When in doubt, use broader trend language instead of a specific unverifiable number. General statements do not require citations. Specific numbers do, and every one must trace to a primary source the writer loaded.
- Named specific examples (companies, studies, institutions) must be verified the same way. If a Deep Research brief says "X, Y, and Z appear on List A," open List A and confirm the names before writing them into a draft.
- The following pattern is a red flag and must be caught at write time: "Research by [Source] shows that [specific number]." If you cannot point to the specific sentence on the rendered page that contains that number, rewrite the claim.
- Home services industry data is acceptable when [YOUR_VERTICAL]-specific data is not available — but note when a stat covers home services broadly vs. [YOUR_VERTICAL] specifically.

## Content Structure

### Default Outline
If no outline is provided, use this structure:
1. **Introduction/Problem Statement** — Hook with a relatable scenario targeting the selected company size tier
2. **Current Industry Context** — Trends and challenges facing [YOUR_VERTICAL] companies
3. **Main Solutions/Points** — 3-5 sections with actionable content
4. **Implementation Steps or Actionable Takeaways**
5. **Practical Application** — Realistic scenario grounded in the target size tier's budget, staffing, and market position (see guidelines below)
6. **Conclusion with CTA** — See `reference/cta-rules.md` for approved CTA phrasing and format
7. **FAQ Section** — 3-5 questions (see FAQ Guidelines below)

### Content Ratios
- Maintain 70% narrative flow, 30% formatted elements (lists, tables, callouts)
- Paragraphs: 2-4 sentences maximum
- Minimum 1 subheading per 300 words
- Use headers naturally for content organization (H2, H3 only; no H4+)
- Include clear transitions between sections

### Heading Format
- Use question-based H2 headings for featured snippet targeting
- Provide a concise answer of approximately 50 words (±20%, so 40-60 words) immediately after each H2
- All headings must be SEO-optimized with keywords
- Headlines under 100 characters
- Headings use AP title case. See the Capitalization section of `reference/mechanical-style-rules.md` for the full rule (single source of truth — do not restate it here)
- Active voice in headings
- NEVER put puns or jokes in headings

### Answer Pattern for Question-Format H2s and H3s
Each question heading should be followed by a structured answer that LLM-based search engines (Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity) can cite as a standalone unit. Follow this pattern:

1. **First sentence: the direct answer.** State the recommendation, definition, or yes/no up front. Do not lead with backstory.
2. **Next 1-3 sentences: detail or reasoning.** Explain how, why, or under what conditions.
3. **Optional: a concrete example or specific number.** Where natural, include a real example or supporting statistic to anchor the answer.

This pattern matters because AI search engines chunk content for citation at the heading-and-answer level. If the answer leads with context instead of the answer, the citation is less likely. It also matters for human readers scanning the page.

Example:
```
## How Can [YOUR_VERTICAL] Companies Improve Their Google Business Profile?
A fully optimized Google Business Profile helps [YOUR_VERTICAL] companies show up in map pack results when homeowners search for local [SERVICE_PROVIDER]s. Companies that complete every section and actively manage reviews typically see 25-40% more calls from local search. The biggest gains come from posting weekly service updates, responding to every review within 48 hours, and adding service-area cities directly in the description.
```

The first sentence answers the question directly. The second adds the supporting outcome. The third gives concrete tactics. A reader, a featured snippet, and an AI search engine can all use this block on its own.

### Standalone Readability Rule
Treat every H2 and H3 section like a standalone unit. If a reader (or an AI tool) pulled just that section out of the article with no surrounding context, would it still make sense? If a section starts with "this approach," "as mentioned above," or "the next step is," it depends on prior context. Restructure so the block stands alone. Add a brief framing sentence at the start if needed.

## Writing Process

### Word Count — CRITICAL COUNTING RULES
- Word count targets apply ONLY to the main blog content: **introduction paragraph through the conclusion/CTA paragraph**
- **STOP COUNTING at the conclusion.** Everything after the conclusion is NOT part of the word count.
- These sections are EXCLUDED from the word count — do not count a single word from any of them:
  - TL;DR section (everything between `{{TLDR}}` and `{{endTLDR}}`)
  - FAQ section (everything between `{{FAQ}}` and `{{endFAQ}}`)
  - Linking suggestions
  - Any tables or reports added by later stages
- If a post targets 1,500 words, that means 1,500 words from the first paragraph of the introduction through the last sentence of the conclusion. The TL;DR and FAQ add 500-800 words on top of that. This is expected and correct.
- Let the content be as long as it needs to be to cover the subject thoroughly, but short enough to keep it interesting
- Do not pad content to hit a target number — every paragraph should earn its place
- If the Stage 1 brief includes a recommended word count, treat it as a guideline, not a hard limit

### Pre-Write Scratchpad — Required Before Drafting
*In batch mode, complete this step silently before writing. Do not output the scratchpad in the draft.*

Before you write the first sentence, build a working checklist. This anchors the draft against the brief and prevents missing required elements when you're deep into prose. Think of this as the carpenter's measure-twice step.

Generate the following internally:

1. **Required elements checklist.** Confirm you can name where each will live in the draft:
   - Introduction with who-we-serve link (per `reference/cta-rules.md`)
   - Practical application scenario grounded in the target size tier
   - Conclusion paragraph with contact-page CTA
   - 3-5 FAQ questions
   - TL;DR if main body exceeds 2,500 words

2. **Persona pain points to weave in.** From `reference/reader-personas.md`, list 3-5 specific pain points the selected reader persona faces. Reference these during drafting so the content addresses what the reader actually cares about — not generic marketing copy.

3. **Prohibited terms to actively avoid.** Open `reference/prohibited-phrases.md` and note any terms that are particularly tempting for this topic. The point isn't to memorize the list — Stage 3 runs a full automated scan against it. The point is to catch the obvious offenders before they appear in the first place. Common drifts in tactical [YOUR_VERTICAL] content include "leverage," "streamline," "in today's landscape," "robust," "unlock," "dive into," and "navigate." If your topic is technology-adjacent (AI, automation, software), watch for "cutting-edge" and "holistic" especially.

4. **Primary keyword placement.** From the Stage 1 brief: keyword in H1 title, first 100 words (±5%), at least one H2.

5. **Answer-pattern targets.** Identify which H2s will be question-format. Each one needs a 40-60 word answer using the direct-answer-first pattern (see Heading Format above).

This scratchpad is internal scaffolding. It does not appear in the published content. Once complete, proceed to the section-by-section approach.

## Style and Formatting Rules

### Mechanical Style (numbers, money, dates, times, abbreviations, web terms)
Follow `reference/mechanical-style-rules.md` for all mechanical formatting: contractions (use them, three exceptions listed there), the number spell-out threshold (one through nine as words, 10 and up as numerals; numerals in headings, stats, and list titles), percentages (numerals plus `%`, body copy included), money, dates, times, days of the week, abbreviations (spell out on first use), and settled web-term spellings (website, email, ecommerce, login/log in). Never start a sentence with a numeral. That file is the single source of truth for these; this stage does not restate them. Its NAP/phone/address rules are scoped to client-site builds and do not apply to blog body copy.

### Core Style
- AP Style throughout with the Oxford comma (exception to AP)
- Active voice preferred
- Reading level: Flesch-Kincaid grade 9 or lower, Flesch Reading Ease 60 or higher. These are ceilings, not targets: easier copy is never a miss. The audience is hands-on operators reading between routes, often on a phone, often during a 10-minute break. Anything above grade 10 reads like a consultant deck and gets bounced. Hit the target by keeping average sentence length under 20 words, using common words over multi-syllable jargon, and breaking long sentences into short ones.
- Professional yet conversational tone
- Solution-focused approach
- Authoritative without being condescending
- Address reader directly when appropriate
- Use concrete examples over abstract concepts
- Speak to these business owners like a peer who happens to know marketing, not like a consultant talking down to them

### Em Dash Rule
Em dashes follow the canon in the Punctuation section of `reference/mechanical-style-rules.md`: maximum 1 per 300 words of body copy, main body only, enforced as a scripts/qc.sh warning. See that file for the full rule, including the writer habit (single source of truth — do not restate it here). The Pre-Save Self-Audit below covers the mechanical count.

### Markdown Formatting
- Consistent header hierarchy (H1 > H2 > H3 only)
- Strategic use of bold and italics
- Lists only when necessary (30% maximum of total content)
- Use asterisks (*) for bullet points, not dashes
- Clear section breaks using headings — NOT horizontal rules
- **Do NOT use horizontal rules (`---` or `***` or `___`) anywhere in the blog content.** No exceptions. Use a new heading to create visual separation between sections.

### Heading vs. Bold Rule — CRITICAL
- NEVER use bold text as a substitute for a heading. If a line of text introduces a new topic, subtopic, or section, it MUST be a proper markdown heading (`##` or `###`).
- **Bold with a period is NOT a heading.** Lines like `**This Is a New Topic.**` followed by a paragraph are wrong. Use `### This Is a New Topic` instead.
- Bold text is for emphasis WITHIN a paragraph (key terms, numbers, critical phrases) — not for introducing new sections.
- Every distinct topic shift needs a proper H2 or H3, not bold text acting as a visual separator.
- When in doubt, use a heading. Too many headings is better than fake headings in bold.

**Wrong:**
`**Why Your [YOUR_VERTICAL] Company Needs a Google Business Profile.**`
A Google Business Profile helps potential customers find your business...

**Right:**
`### Why Your [YOUR_VERTICAL] Company Needs a Google Business Profile`
A Google Business Profile helps potential customers find your business...

### Paragraph Rules
- 2-4 sentences maximum per paragraph
- Short, direct sentences
- Balance tactical marketing advice with practical, budget-aware implementation
- Use 1-2 strong examples rather than exhaustive lists

## In-body image alt text

Suggest an in-body visual roughly every 300 to 500 words, with the first one
near the top. Placement follows meaning, not a word count. Do not invent a
visual for a section that genuinely needs none. Most posts land at 2 to 5
suggestions.

Put them in the draft notes at the end of the file, not in the published body.
One line each:

```
Image suggestion, after H2 "[heading]": [what the image shows]
Alt: [alt text]
```

Alt-text rules:

- Under 125 characters.
- Describe the image accurately for someone who cannot see it.
- Be specific. "Technician kneeling at a crawl space door" beats "a worker."
- No "image of" or "picture of". Screen readers already say it is an image.
- No em dashes. Use commas or periods.
- Work in the target keyword or a natural variant only when it honestly fits
  what the image shows. Never keyword-stuff.

These are **in-body** suggestions only. The cover image and its alt text come
from Stage 10, which reads the finished draft. Do not write a cover suggestion
here.

The full rules live in `blog-image-prompts/reference/alt-text-rules.md`, along
with the purpose tags and the placement cadence.

## Practical Application Guidelines

When illustrating concepts with a scenario or case study:

- Base the example on the target reader's company size tier, using realistic staffing, revenue, budget, and market position from `reference/reader-personas.md`
- Use the fictional companies in `reference/fictional-companies.md` as guides for realistic details at each size tier, but do NOT name them in the content
- **Never use fictional persona names or fictional company names in published content.** No "Mike," "Sarah," "[CLIENT_COMPANY]," "[CLIENT_COMPANY]," etc. These exist only as internal reference guides.
- Use descriptive language instead: "a 3-person startup in a mid-sized market," "an 8-truck operation expanding into a neighboring county," "a 40-technician company competing against national chains"
- Ground examples in realistic metrics and outcomes appropriate to the size tier's budget and resources
- Show before/after scenarios, implementation steps, or results that the reader can map to their own situation

- Home services industry data (plumbing, HVAC, roofing, electrical, landscaping) is acceptable for supporting examples when [YOUR_VERTICAL]-specific data is unavailable — but note when data covers the broader category

## Required Elements Checklist

Every blog post MUST include all of the following:

### 1. TL;DR Section

**Word count for TL;DR threshold is based on main body content only (introduction through conclusion). Do NOT include the FAQ or TL;DR itself in this count.**

**When to include a TL;DR:**
- Count ONLY the main blog content: introduction through conclusion (including the CTA paragraph)
- Do NOT count the FAQ section, TL;DR itself, or linking suggestions toward this threshold
- If the main body content exceeds **2,500 words (±5%)**, a TL;DR is REQUIRED
- If the main body content is **2,000-2,500 words**, a TL;DR is RECOMMENDED but optional
- If the main body content is under **2,000 words (±5%)**, do NOT include a TL;DR

**Word count verification step:** Before deciding whether to include a TL;DR, explicitly count (or estimate) the words in the main body only. State the approximate main body word count in your draft notes. Do not rely on total document length.

- Place near the top, after the introduction
- List the critical takeaways (not a summary of the whole post)
- **NO links in the TL;DR — none, ever**
- MUST use this exact CMS template tag format:

```
### TL;DR
{{TLDR}}

[TL;DR bullet points here]

## Introduction: [Short SEO Title, Under 80 Characters]
{{endTLDR}}

[Introduction paragraph starts here — outside the wrapper]
```

Example:
```
## Introduction: Why [YOUR_VERTICAL] Companies Need a Local SEO Strategy
```

The `{{TLDR}}` and `{{endTLDR}}` tags are required for CMS automation. The introduction heading sits inside the wrapper so it doesn't show in the blog feed. The introduction paragraph sits outside the wrapper so it's visible as the start of the post.

The introduction heading should be a brief, SEO-focused title that summarizes the post in under 80 characters. It does not need to match the SEO, Article, or Image titles from Stage 4 — it's its own thing. Keep the primary keyword in it when natural. This is an H2 heading, not a sentence or paragraph.

### 2. Introduction
- Start with `## Introduction: [Short SEO Title]` as the heading — under 80 characters, not a sentence (this heading sits inside the TL;DR wrapper)
- The introduction paragraph sits immediately after the `{{endTLDR}}` tag — outside the wrapper
- Hook with a relatable scenario that targets the selected size tier's pain points
- Establish the problem clearly
- Preview what the post will cover

### 3. Main Content
- Practical, actionable advice supported by verified research
- Statistics cited using the 5-format rotation system
- Practical application scenario grounded in the target size tier's company profile (see Practical Application Guidelines above)

### 4. Conclusion with CTA
- Summarize key actionable points
- End with call-to-action using the format specified in `reference/cta-rules.md`

### 5. FAQ Section
- 3-5 questions related to the content topic
- Format as H3 headers for questions with concise paragraph answers
- Questions based on common searches or concerns of [YOUR_VERTICAL] business owners
- Optimize for search engines and featured snippets
- Cover different aspects of the main topic
- Use natural language (how people actually ask)
- MUST use this exact CMS template tag format with H3 headers for questions and answers directly below:

```
## Frequently Asked Questions
{{FAQ}}

### This Is the First FAQ Question?
This is the direct answer to the question. Keep it concise and actionable.

### This Is the Second FAQ Question?
And this is the answer to that question. No blank lines or extra formatting between the H3 and the answer text.

{{endFAQ}}
```

The `{{FAQ}}` and `{{endFAQ}}` tags are required for CMS automation.

### 6. Linking Suggestions
After the FAQ closing tag (`{{endFAQ}}`), add an H1 heading to mark the start of the links section:
```
# Links
```

All link-related content from Stages 6, 7, and 8 will be placed under this heading. The `# Links` H1 must always exist between the FAQ section and any link tables or suggestions.

At the end of the draft, provide:
- 3-5 internal linking opportunities (suggest where [YOUR_AGENCY] blog posts could be linked)
- 3-5 external linking opportunities (authoritative sources that strengthen credibility)


### 7. Word Count Verification
Before finalizing the draft, verify the word count by counting ONLY from the introduction paragraph through the conclusion/CTA paragraph. Report this number as the draft word count. Do not include the TL;DR, FAQ, or any linking suggestions in this count.

### 8. Pre-Save Self-Audit — Required Before Saving Draft

Before saving the draft, run the following checks. This is not a vague "review your work" pass — it is a specific list of verifications, each with a defined action. Document your findings briefly in the draft notes (not in the published content).

**Optional sanity check:** If you have a QC script configured, run `scripts/qc.sh` against the draft before saving:

```bash
bash scripts/qc.sh output/[topic-slug]-draft.md
```

This prints a markdown report showing prohibited-phrase hits, readability metrics, and structural violations. Stage 3 runs this as a required step — running it here as well catches issues at the cheapest moment to fix them. The full audit checklist below still applies (the script does not cover the semantic checks in steps 4-6).

1. **Em-dash count.** Search the draft for em dashes (—). Count them. Take the main body word count from step 7. The ratio must not exceed 1 em dash per 300 words. If it does, replace excess em dashes with semicolons, periods, or parentheses.

2. **Flesch-Kincaid estimate.** Sample 2-3 paragraphs from different sections. Estimate the grade level using sentence length and syllable density. Target: Flesch-Kincaid grade 9 or lower, Flesch Reading Ease 60 or higher. These are ceilings, not targets: easier copy is never a miss. If average sentence length exceeds 22 words or paragraphs are loaded with multi-syllable jargon, simplify. The audience is operators reading on a phone between jobs, not academics with time.

3. **Heading-vs-bold scan.** Search the draft for lines matching the pattern `**Text.**` followed by a paragraph (bold-as-heading violation). Convert each to a proper `###` heading.

4. **Standalone readability check.** For each H2 and H3 section, ask: if a reader pulled this section out of context — or an AI search engine cited just this block — would it still make sense? If a section depends on prior context (starts with "this approach," "as mentioned above," "the next step"), add a brief framing sentence so the block stands alone. **This check requires semantic judgment — no automated script can do it for you.**

5. **H2 question + answer-pattern check.** For each question-format H2, confirm: (a) the answer follows immediately after the heading, (b) it's approximately 50 words (±20%, so 40-60 words), (c) the first sentence is the direct answer — not backstory, not context. Restructure if the answer is buried. **This check requires semantic judgment.**

6. **Required-elements completeness.** Confirm each item from the Pre-Write Scratchpad checklist is present in the final draft. If anything is missing, add it before saving.

7. **Prohibited-phrase awareness pass.** Stage 3 runs the full automated scan via `scripts/qc.sh` (if configured). At this stage, spot-check for the obvious offenders that crept in despite the scratchpad: leverage, streamline, dive into, in today's, holistic, robust, unlock, delve, navigate, landscape, cutting-edge, utilize, foster, harness. Fix any you find. Stage 3 catches the rest.

If any check fails, fix it before saving. The cost of catching issues here is a few minutes. The cost of catching them after Stage 8 is rerunning multiple stages.
